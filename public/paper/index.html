Null result · Quantitative trading

Thirty-One Signals, Six Bets

A retail account cannot assemble enough independent bets to detect an edge it might actually have — and adding more price signals does not help.

Universe 112 US large caps, 110 small caps Period Sep 2016 – Sep 2026 Capital $100,000 simulated Tests 372 passing

Abstract

A Wall Street Journal profile described an investor delegating capital to named LLM agents. We built a purpose-designed backtesting and paper-trading harness to ask whether that idea, or any of its rules-based neighbours, survives honest measurement in a retail-sized account. Nine experiments were run over a decade of daily bars: two LLM agent designs, adaptive strategy selection, transaction-cost measurement, a small-cap capacity test, a turnover sweep, a pre-registered out-of-sample test, a market-neutral construction, and a breadth study.

No configuration beat buy-and-hold. The central result is structural rather than circumstantial: 31 signals drawn from six families of the published anomaly literature collapse to 5.6–6.2 independent bets. Zero signals reach |t| ≥ 2 in large caps and one does in small caps, against 1.6 expected by chance. The combined information coefficient is negative in both universes. At the measured IC, a usable information ratio requires roughly 1,300 independent bets per year; 31 price-derived signals supply about 67.

The shortfall is two orders of magnitude, so it is not closed by writing more signals from the same price series. We also argue that the one remaining path — LLM-extracted features from text — cannot be backtested honestly, because the contamination lives in the model's weights rather than in the data handed to it.

§ 1

The question, and why it needed a harness

The prompt was a newspaper story: a private investor running money through several LLM agents, each with a name and a personality. The obvious first move is to build the agents. We built the measuring apparatus first, because the interesting failure mode of this idea is not that the agents lose money — it is that a badly-built backtest will say they made money.

Everything below rests on five properties of the harness. Each exists because its absence produces a specific, well-documented lie.

Property 1Look-ahead is prevented structurally, not by discipline

A strategy is handed a MarketView pinned to one date. It holds no reference to the underlying series and exposes exactly five members: close, history, ret, symbols, today. Reading tomorrow is not something to remember not to do; there is no method that returns it. A test asserts the entire public surface, so adding one would break the build.

Property 2The backtest runs the live risk gate

A backtest that gates differently from production is testing something other than what will run. The same pure function evaluate(proposal, context, limits, universe) judges every order in replay and in the paper account. The replay engine satisfies the gate's paper-account check with an account id of DU-REPLAY rather than by branching around it.

Property 3Decisions at close of t, fills against t+1

Accepted orders enter a pending list and are only touched on the next iteration. Unfilled day orders expire, matching the tif="DAY" the broker actually sends.

Property 4Survivorship is controlled by construction

Both universes were assembled from memory in 2026 and are therefore stacked with survivors. The benchmark is not an index — it is equal-weighting the same names, which carries exactly the same bias. A strategy that cannot beat equal-weighting its own universe is not being helped by survivorship; it is worse than not choosing. The size of the problem is visible: equal-weighting the hand-picked large caps beat SPY by 150 points, and the small caps beat IWM by 226.

Property 5Costs are measured, not assumed

Quoted half-spreads were sampled from the live feed across both universes. Cost sensitivity is computed by holding the realised order sequence fixed and recharging it, never by re-running the strategy — re-running changes one fill, which changes every later position, and the resulting rows are not comparable. The anchoring is verified: the reference assumption reproduces the realised return to within 0.04 points (+367.94% against +367.90%).

§ 2

Nine experiments

Each was run because the previous one left a specific objection standing. They are reported in the order they were run, including the ones that failed for reasons we had not anticipated.

2.1 · LLM as pickerThe agent was momentum wearing a hat

The first agent, Scout, saw price-derived features for the whole universe and returned names to hold. Its prompt deliberately carried returns and volatility only — no sector, no market capitalisation, no company names — so that any drift toward mega-caps would be attributable to the model's priors rather than to the prompt. A test asserts none of those fields leak into the rendered table.

It did not need a full backtest to fail. Every visible pick sat inside the momentum strategy's own top 20, because four of its five inputs were momentum features. An agent can only express what its inputs allow. This is a design result, not a performance result, and it is the reason the second design exists.

2.2 · LLM as veto−111.9 points, and it removed the better half

The second design wraps any strategy: the strategy proposes, the agent may drop some buys, and it may do nothing else. It cannot add a name, resize one, or veto a sell. This is cleanly measurable (identical order flow with and without), has bounded downside (the worst it can do is leave you in cash), and asks something a cross-sectional rank structurally cannot answer — its prompt carries market context: index return, breadth, median volatility.

463 rebalances, zero model failures, $9.48 of API spend.

It vetoed the wrong names: over the following five sessions the vetoed candidates returned +0.633% against +0.343% for the kept ones — it systematically removed the better half (t = +1.38, not significant, but pointing the wrong way). It never adapted: the veto rate sat between 16.7% and 21.2% in every single year, with 2022 indistinguishable from 2021. Sold as regime awareness, delivered as a flat ~19% haircut that a rule could apply for free.

2.4 · Transaction costs2.40 bps large cap, 14.27 bps small cap

Quoted half-spreads, 112 names, three rounds, on the live feed. Median 1.91 bps; trade-weighted for what momentum actually buys, 2.40. The 1.5 bps default in the execution model was too low.

Three limits on what this proves, all stated because they cut the same way: these are quoted spreads, an upper bound on effective cost; they are today's spreads applied to a nine-year history, and spreads have generally narrowed; and the feed is delayed 15–20 minutes.

2.6 · TurnoverCosts stop mattering; the signal was never there

Twelve configurations per universe (lookback 60/120/250 × hold 21/63/126/252). Turnover falls from 22–30× to 0.6–3.6×, and in small caps the spread drag collapses from 43 points to under 2. The binding constraint is removed.

What it reveals is a random field. Excess over equal-weight ranges from −228 to +225 points with a standard deviation of ~129, and the largest swing from a single parameter step is 302 points. A real effect produces a gradient; this does not. The standout cell (+225%, or +4.82%/yr) was chosen from twelve draws whose expected maximum under a pure-noise null sits about 210 points above the mean — the observed 225 is almost exactly what noise looks like once sorted.

2.8 · Market-neutralThe construction worked; the argument did not

Every result so far carried the market factor: one giant correlated bet whose variance swamps everything else. The stated rationale was explicit and falsifiable — at a 4%/yr excess, 16% volatility gives IR 0.25 and needs 64 years to reach t = 2, while 6% volatility gives IR 0.67 and needs 9. Same alpha; removing variance nobody is paying for was supposed to be the highest-leverage change available.

Beta collapsed from 0.96 to roughly zero — the construction does what it says. The variance reduction did not follow. Volatility stayed near 20% instead of falling toward 6%, because the short leg cancels the market factor while contributing its own idiosyncratic risk: you replace one correlated bet with forty individual ones. The arithmetic that justified the whole exercise does not survive its own test.

The alpha is also negative — shorting the bottom of a momentum ranking through a decade-long bull market loses money — and three of five books hit margin calls.

One constraint is worth recording because it makes this permanently a measurement rather than a strategy: short selling is not available in an IRA, the natural home for an account like this. The long/short book exists to answer "is there alpha here at all" with the statistical power to see it, not to be deployed.

§ 3

The central result: 31 signals are 6 bets

What survived the neutral construction is the other half of Grinold's fundamental law. IR = IC × √breadth pays the square root only if the bets are independent. Thirty-one signals that all say "buy what went up" are one bet with thirty spare copies.

We built 31 signals across six declared families — momentum, reversal, volatility, liquidity, technical, shape — from the published anomaly literature. Each states the prior fixing its sign before seeing data, because choosing signs afterwards is 31 free parameters and would fit anything. Two pairs are deliberate near-duplicates, planted as positive controls for the correlation matrix.

Controls before conclusions

The instrument was tested against a signal that is the forward return (must measure IC ≈ 1) and against pure noise (must measure IC ≈ 0 and effective breadth ≈ the signal count). An instrument that cannot see a perfect edge cannot be trusted when it reports none, and one that finds structure in noise would flatter every result in this paper.

A note on precision: the large-cap agreement (2.22 against 2.36) is closer than this statistic deserves. Its denominator is a mean IC near −0.005, close enough to zero that the ratio is unstable, and the small-cap replication gives 3.71 against 2.49. The directional claim survives; the apparent 6% accuracy was luck.

§ 4

The arithmetic that ends it

At the measured mean |IC| of 0.014, the breadth needed for a usable information ratio follows directly from IR = IC × √BR:

Table 9 — What the measured breadth supports
ConfigurationBets/yrIRYears to t = 2
5.57 breadth × monthly rebalance670.11305
5.57 breadth × weekly rebalance2900.2470
8.40 breadth × weekly rebalance4370.2947

Weekly rebalancing was already eliminated in §2.6: at that turnover the measured spreads consume the edge. So the honest row is the first one.

Going from 3 signals to 31 bought an effective breadth of 5.57. The marginal signal is worth about 0.2 of a bet. Closing a two-orders-of-magnitude gap by continuing down that path would require thousands of additions, each less independent than the last. This is not a statement that our search was insufficiently thorough. It is a statement about the slope.

There is also a structural reason it had to come out this way. Every signal in the catalogue is computable by anyone with a CSV of daily bars and has been for thirty years.

§ 5

Errors found, and why they are the evidence

A null result is only worth reading if the apparatus that produced it was capable of producing a positive one. The strongest available evidence for that is the list of errors the harness caught — several of which would have manufactured an edge had they survived.

  1. 01Split double-adjustment, wrong by 40× Yahoo's quote values are already split-adjusted. Applying a split factor divided NVDA's pre-split history by 40 twice. Caught by a sanity check requiring split-only history to sit above dividend-adjusted history; a regression test now fails if the committed file ever shows a split-shaped gap.
  2. 02The test suite silently ran 110 of 141 tests An unterminated f-string meant a module would not import, so unittest discover collected a subset — and still printed OK.
  3. 03A commit landed with failing tests A tail in a shell pipeline masked the exit code. Commits are now gated on the suite's actual status.
  4. 04Preflight reported "all checks passed" on an API that could not place orders Read-only mode was enabled. An explicit order-permission probe was added.
  5. 05A paper-only guard that guarded nothing The port check was described as one of three independent safeguards; the gateway in fact served live accounts on that port. The account-prefix assertion is the only load-bearing check, and the documentation was corrected to say so.
  6. 06Path divergence invalidated a cost sweep Raising the assumed half-spread increased measured excess, because one changed fill changes every later position. Cost sensitivity was rebuilt to hold order flow fixed.
  7. 07A recorder double-called a stateful strategy This desynchronised the rebalance cadence and silently produced an empty sample.
  8. 08The market-neutral book went $103k net short on $100k Dollar neutrality decays asymmetrically — a losing short grows as a share of the book while a losing long shrinks. NAV reached −$12,091 because the simulator had no margin logic at all. Both were fixed; forced liquidations are now recorded, and any non-zero count means the run hit a wall a real account would have hit.
  9. 09Limits measuring themselves, five or six times Scoring a strategy under limits that forbid its trades measures the limits. Each occurrence is documented in place.
  10. 10An overstated claim, corrected by replication The combination-lift agreement was reported as accurate "within 6%" and called the cleanest result in the project. The small-cap run showed the statistic is unstable. See §3.3.

Items 01, 06, 07 and 08 would each have produced a flattering number. Item 10 is included because the correction arrived from a replication we had already committed to running, not from a reviewer.

§ 6

Limitations

  • One decade, one regime. September 2016 to September 2026 contains a historic bull market and two short drawdowns. A momentum book that fails here might not fail in a different regime, and a short leg that loses money here is being asked to fight the trend of the whole sample.
  • Survivorship is controlled, not eliminated. Equal-weighting the same universe removes the bias from the comparison. It does not make the universes representative — the construction premia of +150 and +226 points show how far from representative they are.
  • Daily bars only. No intraday data, and no market-on-close or market-on-open execution. The overnight/intraday decomposition we measured (SPY: +9.10%/yr overnight against +3.98% intraday, Sharpe 0.81 vs 0.35) is deliberately not wired into the harness, because capturing it needs execution the daily-bar engine cannot represent.
  • Spreads are quoted, current, and delayed. Measured in 2026 and applied to a nine-year history.
  • Thirty-one signals is not "all signals." It is 31 price-derived signals. The breadth result is about that class, and the paper's conclusion is scoped to it.
  • The IC study is in-sample across the whole decade. That is deliberate — it is a decision about whether to keep building, not a strategy — but it means the null is a null on the most favourable possible reading. In-sample failure is dispositive in a way in-sample success never is.
  • One implementation, one author. No independent replication.
§ 7

What would change the answer, and why it cannot be tested

The only thing that moves this result is genuinely orthogonal information — not another transformation of the same OHLCV series. The obvious candidate is text: earnings-call language, guidance changes, filings. Unlike the price path, its arithmetic is not absurd.

Ten years to significance is a different universe from three hundred and five. That is a real difference in kind, and it is the one place an LLM could plausibly contribute — as a source of features, not as a decision maker over the same prices it has already been shown to reproduce.

But it cannot be backtested honestly. A model trained through 2025 already knows what happened after a 2019 earnings call.

This is worth stating precisely because it defeats the specific defence this project relies on everywhere else. Our look-ahead prevention is structural: the strategy holds an object that has no method returning the future. That defence is useless here, because the contamination is not in the data handed to the model — it is in the weights. There is no held-out set, no windowing scheme, and no prompt discipline that removes it. A model can be instructed not to use hindsight; it cannot be made not to have it.

So the only valid test of the text path is forward, on paper, over years, on data that must be licensed. That is not an argument that it will not work. It is the price of finding out, and it should be paid knowingly rather than discovered after a backtest that was never capable of being honest.

§ 8

Conclusion

Nine experiments, two LLM agent designs, two universes, a decade of daily bars, and 372 tests produced no configuration that beat buy-and-hold. That alone would be unremarkable — most such projects end this way, and most of them end without knowing why.

The contribution here is the why, and it is measurable rather than anecdotal. The limiting factor is not signal quality: an IC of 0.014–0.020 is an ordinary quantitative IC, and professionals build viable products from less. The limiting factor is breadth. Thirty-one signals drawn from six distinct-sounding families of the anomaly literature deliver 5.6 independent bets, and at a monthly rebalance that supports an information ratio of 0.11 — three hundred years to statistical significance.

Successful quantitative funds do not solve this with better mathematics on closing prices. They solve it with breadth: thousands of instruments across asset classes, data that is not public, and institutional financing applied to a small edge. A retail account can replicate their mathematics exactly. It cannot replicate their bet count, and the mathematics is worthless without it.

The apparatus outlived the hypothesis. It caught a 40× data error, a test suite silently running two-thirds of its tests, a safety check that checked nothing, a simulator that permitted negative equity, and — twice — a claim of ours that was stronger than the evidence. That is what it was built for, and it is the part worth keeping.
